Section 01

The Problem

Food insecurity is one of the largest preventable humanitarian crises on Earth. Yet the tools available to predict and prevent it are fragmented, delayed, and inaccessible to the people who need them most.

828M
People face food insecurity globally every day
70%
Of the world's food is produced by smallholder farmers
45
Countries currently face acute food crises (WFP 2025)

What makes food crises hard to predict?

Food insecurity is caused by the interaction of multiple factors — drought, conflict, economic collapse, supply chain disruption, and pest outbreaks. No single data stream tells the full story. A drought in Kenya looks different from a conflict-driven crisis in Yemen, even if both result in the same IPC phase. Historically, by the time a famine is officially declared, it has already been unfolding for months.

What tools existed before HarvestGuard?

🗺 WFP HungerMap

Shows current food security status. No forecasting. No 30-60 day outlook. No satellite data integration. A snapshot, not a warning system.

📋 FEWS NET Reports

Expert monthly reports with food security outlooks. No public API. No real-time data. Reports can be weeks old before they're acted upon.

🛰 NASA EarthData

Raw satellite data (NDVI, rainfall). Requires significant technical expertise to download, process, and interpret. Not accessible to field workers or NGOs.

📰 News & Reports

By the time a food crisis makes the news, it has already peaked. Reactive, not proactive. No standardised signal that triggers early response.

⚡
The Core Gap

No open, publicly accessible platform exists that combines real-time satellite vegetation data, rainfall anomalies, humanitarian field indicators, and AI-driven natural language analysis into a single early warning system. HarvestGuard fills that gap.

Section 02

The IPC Classification System

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) is the globally accepted standard for measuring the severity of food insecurity. Every metric in HarvestGuard ultimately maps to one of these five phases.

Phase Name Definition Key Indicators Required Response
1 Minimal Households can meet essential food and non-food needs without engaging in atypical coping strategies. Normal food access, adequate diet diversity, low acute malnutrition. Monitoring only. No immediate intervention needed.
2 Stressed Households have minimally adequate food consumption but are struggling to afford basic needs, or are using borderline coping strategies. Selling productive assets, dietary changes, reduced meals. Prevention programs, livelihood support, monitoring.
3 Crisis Households have food consumption gaps that are reflected in high or above-usual acute malnutrition, or are marginally able to meet minimum food needs. Skipping meals, children showing malnutrition signs, selling livestock. Urgent action needed. Food assistance, nutrition interventions.
4 Emergency Households have large food consumption gaps resulting in very high acute malnutrition and excess mortality. Or are barely able to meet absolute minimum food needs by depleting essential assets. Severe acute malnutrition, death from starvation, displacement for food. Emergency response. Immediate food aid, medical intervention.
5 Catastrophe / Famine Households have an extreme lack of food and/or other basic needs even after full employment of coping strategies. Starvation, death, destitution, and extremely critical acute malnutrition levels are evident. Famine declared. Starvation mortality. Complete destitution. Maximum emergency. International mobilisation required.
ℹ️
Who decides IPC phases?

IPC phases are determined by multi-agency technical working groups including WFP, FAO, UNICEF, and government nutrition experts. They analyse field surveys, satellite data, market prices, and displacement records. HarvestGuard uses WFP HungerMap's IPC phase data as one of three inputs into the composite risk score, supplemented by satellite signals that can detect deterioration earlier than field surveys.

Section 03

Core Terminology Glossary

Every acronym, technical term, and domain-specific concept used in HarvestGuard — explained in plain language.

NDVI
Normalized Difference Vegetation Index

A satellite-derived measure of vegetation health. Calculated as (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red) using near-infrared and red light reflectance. Values range from −1 to +1. Above 0.3 indicates healthy green vegetation; below 0.1 indicates bare soil, drought stress, or crop failure. HarvestGuard uses 20-year NDVI baselines to detect anomalies.

NDVI Anomaly (σ)
Standard Deviation from 20-Year Mean

How far the current NDVI is from the historical average for that location and time of year, measured in standard deviations (σ). An anomaly of −2σ means vegetation is significantly below normal — a strong crop stress signal. HarvestGuard colours this metric red/amber/green based on severity.

EVI
Enhanced Vegetation Index

A refinement of NDVI that reduces atmospheric distortion and soil background effects. More accurate in regions with high biomass (dense forests) or dust/haze. NASA's MOD13A2 product provides both NDVI and EVI. HarvestGuard uses EVI mean for its zonal statistics calculations.

MODIS
Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer

A NASA sensor aboard the Terra and Aqua satellites. Captures global imagery every 1–2 days at 250m–1km resolution across 36 spectral bands. One of the most important instruments for global vegetation and climate monitoring since 1999. HarvestGuard uses MODIS MOD13A2 (16-day NDVI/EVI composite).

MOD13A2
MODIS Terra Vegetation Indices 16-Day Global 1km

A specific NASA data product that composites 16 days of MODIS observations into a single vegetation index grid, removing cloud contamination. Each pixel is 1km × 1km. HarvestGuard downloads these tiles, clips them to country boundaries, and computes the average EVI/NDVI per country.

CHIRPS
Climate Hazards Infrared Precipitation with Stations

A monthly rainfall dataset from UC Santa Barbara that blends satellite infrared estimates with ground station measurements. Available globally from 1981. HarvestGuard uses CHIRPS monthly totals to compute how much a country's rainfall deviates from the 1981–2010 historical baseline (the "anomaly %" metric).

Rainfall Anomaly %
Percentage Deviation from Historical Baseline

How much a month's rainfall differs from the long-term average for that region and month. −30% means 30% less rain than normal — a serious drought signal. +20% could indicate flooding risk. The 1981–2010 period is used as the global baseline for CHIRPS comparisons.

WFP
World Food Programme

The United Nations agency mandated to fight hunger worldwide. WFP operates in 123 countries and is the world's largest humanitarian organisation. HarvestGuard consumes WFP's public HungerMap API to retrieve IPC phase classifications and affected population counts per country in near-real-time.

HungerMap
WFP HungerMap Live

WFP's public platform showing near-real-time food security indicators. Provides IPC phase per country, number of people with insufficient food consumption, and trend data. HarvestGuard queries the HungerMap API (api.hungermapdata.org/v2/) every 6 hours to keep data current.

FEWS NET
Famine Early Warning Systems Network

A USAID-funded network that provides food security outlooks for ~35 of the most vulnerable countries. Publishes monthly expert reports combining satellite, market, and field data. Unlike HarvestGuard, FEWS NET has no public real-time API — reports are PDFs released monthly with 2–4 week lag.

IPC
Integrated Food Security Phase Classification

The globally accepted 5-phase scale (1=Minimal to 5=Famine) for classifying food insecurity severity. Developed jointly by FAO, WFP, UNICEF, NGOs, and governments. Used by all major humanitarian organisations to trigger response. See Section 2 for the full breakdown of each phase.

Zonal Statistics
Geographic Aggregation over a Boundary

The process of computing summary statistics (mean, sum, max) of a raster dataset (like a satellite image) within a defined geographic boundary (like a country border). HarvestGuard uses Python's rasterio and GeoPandas to clip MODIS/CHIRPS raster files to country polygons and compute the mean vegetation index per country.

Smallholder Farmer
Small-Scale Agricultural Producer

Farmers who cultivate less than 2 hectares of land, typically using family labour with limited technology access. They collectively produce 70% of the world's food supply. They are also the most vulnerable to climate shocks, conflict, and market disruption — and have zero access to advance crop failure warnings. HarvestGuard's primary beneficiary.

Affected Population
People in IPC Phase 3, 4, or 5

The number of people within a country experiencing Crisis, Emergency, or Catastrophe-level food insecurity. Sourced from WFP HungerMap. HarvestGuard displays this count in the country detail sidebar. A country can have millions in Phase 3 but still have a lower overall score if only a fraction of the population is affected.

Crop Stress
Vegetation Under Environmental Pressure

When crops or vegetation show physiological strain due to drought, flooding, heat, pests, or soil degradation. Detectable via satellite as a drop in NDVI/EVI values. Crop stress signals lead food crises by 4–8 weeks — the most valuable early warning window HarvestGuard is designed to capture.

Trend
30-Day Score Direction

Whether a country's composite risk score is improving (↓), stable (→), or deteriorating (↑) compared to 30 days ago. A country currently at Phase 2 but deteriorating rapidly may need more urgent attention than a Phase 3 country that is improving. Trend is calculated from the change in composite risk score over the prior month.

Section 04

Real Data Sources

HarvestGuard uses zero synthetic data. Every number shown comes from one of four publicly accessible real-world data sources — all free to access.

🛰

NASA MODIS — Vegetation Health (NDVI/EVI)

Product: MOD13A2 · 16-day global composite · 1km resolution · Free via NASA Earthdata

The Terra satellite completes a full orbit of Earth every 99 minutes. Every 16 days, NASA composites all cloud-free observations into a single global grid of vegetation indices. HarvestGuard downloads the relevant tiles for each of the 61 monitored countries, clips them to the country boundary using PostGIS geometry, and computes the mean EVI value. This value is compared against the 20-year average for the same region and time of year to produce the NDVI anomaly in standard deviations.

Refresh rate: Every 16 days (synced to MODIS composite cycle). Latency: ~5 days after acquisition. What it tells us: Is vegetation healthier or worse than normal? Are crops stressed?

🌧

CHIRPS — Rainfall Anomalies

UC Santa Barbara Climate Hazards Center · Monthly GeoTIFF rasters · 0.05° resolution (~5km) · Freely downloadable

CHIRPS blends infrared satellite imagery (which detects cold cloud tops — a proxy for rain) with 23,000+ ground rain gauge stations globally. The result is a near-continuous global rainfall record going back to 1981. HarvestGuard downloads the monthly GeoTIFF file for the previous month, computes the country-level mean using rasterio, and compares it to the 1981–2010 baseline for that month and location to compute the anomaly percentage.

Refresh rate: Monthly (published ~5 days after month-end). What it tells us: Is a country in drought or flood conditions relative to historical norms?

🌐

WFP HungerMap API — IPC Phase & Population

World Food Programme · REST API · No authentication required · api.hungermapdata.org/v2/

WFP's HungerMap aggregates field surveys, market monitoring, displacement tracking, and nutrition assessments into country-level IPC phase classifications. These are updated continuously as new data becomes available. HarvestGuard polls the HungerMap API every 6 hours to keep phase and affected population data current. This is the "ground truth" humanitarian signal — the satellite data provides the early warning, the IPC phase provides the current severity.

Refresh rate: Every 6 hours. What it tells us: Official humanitarian severity rating (1–5) and how many people are affected.

☁️

Open-Meteo — Weather Forecast & Historical Fill

Open-Meteo.com · Free REST API · No API key · Hourly/daily resolution · archive-api.open-meteo.com

Open-Meteo aggregates weather model outputs (ERA5 reanalysis, ECMWF, GFS) into a freely accessible API. HarvestGuard uses it for two purposes: (1) filling in historical monthly precipitation when CHIRPS data is delayed, and (2) providing 14-day precipitation forecasts that can give an additional forward-looking signal about upcoming drought or flood conditions. No API key required — completely free for non-commercial use.

Refresh rate: Daily historical, real-time forecast. What it tells us: What weather is coming in the next 14 days? Is a drought about to worsen?

Section 05

How HarvestGuard Solves It

HarvestGuard is a five-stage data pipeline that transforms raw satellite signals and humanitarian data into actionable 30-60 day early warnings, surfaced through an AI-driven interface.

1

Data Ingestion — Automated, Scheduled

Three background jobs run continuously. WFP HungerMap is queried every 6 hours for the latest IPC phases. CHIRPS rainfall rasters are downloaded on the 5th of each month when the previous month's data becomes available. NASA MODIS NDVI tiles are fetched every 16 days. All data is stored in PostgreSQL with full history retained — no data is overwritten, enabling trend analysis over time.

2

Satellite Processing — Zonal Statistics per Country

Raw MODIS and CHIRPS data arrive as global raster grids (GeoTIFF images). Python's rasterio library clips each raster to the country's geometry polygon stored in PostGIS. The mean pixel value within the boundary becomes the country's EVI or rainfall reading. This is then compared against the 20-year historical baseline for that country, month, and data type to produce the anomaly value.

3

Risk Scoring — Composite 0–1 Score

Each country receives a composite risk score from 0.0 (minimal risk) to 1.0 (catastrophic). The score blends three signals: vegetation health anomaly (35% weight), rainfall anomaly (30% weight), and WFP IPC phase (35% weight). Each factor is normalised to a 0–1 scale before blending. The resulting score maps directly to IPC phase colours on the map. See Section 6 for the exact formula.

4

Alert Detection — Automated Deterioration Signals

After each scoring cycle, the alert engine compares each country's current score to its score 30 days ago. An increase greater than 0.10 (10 percentage points) triggers an alert. Alerts are severity-tiered: "Watch" (score increased 0.10–0.19), "Warning" (0.20–0.29), and "Emergency" (≥0.30 increase). Alerts appear in the scrolling ticker bar at the top of the HarvestGuard interface and are navigable — clicking an alert focuses the map on that country.

5

AI Analysis — Claude-Powered Natural Language Insights

For every country click, HarvestGuard streams a Claude AI assessment that synthesises the actual satellite numbers into plain-language analysis: what the current data shows, what's driving it, and what interventions are recommended in the next 30–60 days. The AI has the real numbers in its context — it doesn't hallucinate generic advice but references the actual NDVI anomaly, rainfall deficit, IPC phase, and trend for that specific country. The chat interface lets users ask follow-up questions with the selected country's data pre-loaded as context.

Section 06

The Risk Score — Deep Dive

The composite risk score is the single number that summarises a country's food insecurity outlook. Here's exactly how it's computed from raw data.

Step 1 — Normalise each signal to 0–1

# NDVI component — how far below normal is vegetation?
# −3σ or worse = maximum (1.0). Positive anomaly = 0.
ndvi_component = max(0, min(1, −ndvi_anomaly / 3.0))

# Rainfall component — how far below baseline is rainfall?
# −50% or worse = maximum (1.0). Positive anomaly = 0.
rainfall_component = max(0, min(1, −rainfall_anomaly_pct / 50.0))

# IPC phase component — phase 5 = 1.0, phase 1 = 0.0
phase_component = (ipc_phase − 1) / 4.0

# Weighted composite
composite_score = (
  ndvi_component    × 0.35 + # Vegetation health
  rainfall_component × 0.30 + # Rainfall anomaly
  phase_component    × 0.35 # WFP IPC phase
)

💡
Why combine satellite data with IPC phases?

Satellite signals (NDVI, rainfall) can detect agricultural stress 4–8 weeks before it translates into IPC phase upgrades, because IPC phases are determined by field surveys that happen monthly or less frequently. By blending both, HarvestGuard can issue early warnings when satellite signals deteriorate even before the official phase changes — which is precisely the early warning window that saves lives.

Section 08

Who Uses HarvestGuard

The platform is designed to serve anyone who needs to understand, predict, or respond to food insecurity — from satellite analysts to field workers.

🏛

UN Agencies & WFP

Monitor deterioration across all 61 countries simultaneously. Use AI assessments to draft situation reports faster. The API layer enables programmatic integration with existing OCHA/WFP systems.

🌍

National Governments

Ministries of Agriculture and Disaster Management can monitor crop stress signals in near-real-time and pre-position national food reserves before harvests fail.

🤝

NGOs & INGOs

Save the Children, Oxfam, IRC, and similar organisations can use HarvestGuard to prioritise where to deploy field teams and food aid based on objective satellite signals.

🔬

Researchers & Academics

Food security researchers can access the REST API programmatically to retrieve time-series NDVI, rainfall, and risk score data for academic analysis without building their own pipeline.

📰

Journalists

Data journalists covering humanitarian crises can use HarvestGuard to find data-driven stories — which countries are deteriorating fastest, how this season compares to last year.

👨‍🌾

Farmer Networks

Agricultural extension officers and cooperative leaders can use the map on a mobile browser to share 30-day rainfall and vegetation outlooks with smallholder farmer communities.

🎯
The Core Value Proposition

HarvestGuard does not replace FEWS NET experts or WFP field teams. It democratises access to the satellite signals those experts use — making them available to anyone, in real time, through a conversational AI interface that translates raw numbers into actionable plain-language analysis. The early warning window it provides — 4 to 8 weeks ahead of IPC phase upgrades — is where lives can be saved.
